# Evidence that 10-K / 10-Q narrative text predicts returns, earnings, volatility and corporate events

Scope note for the report writer: this file covers US EDGAR filings only (Form 10-K Items 1, 1A, 3, 7; Form 10-Q risk-factor updates; the auditor's report / CAMs). Research-environment constraint: most publisher hosts (SSRN, NBER, arXiv, Wiley, Elsevier, Springer, university repositories, Quantpedia, QuantConnect) were blocked by the egress proxy, so most quantitative claims below come from search-result extracts of those papers rather than from reading the full PDFs. Two documents were read in full (the Filzen–McBrayer–Shannon 10-Q risk-factor-update paper hosted on sec.gov, and the SEC's 2020 Reg S-K release), plus several open GitHub replications. Where a number is from a secondary extract, it is flagged "(per search extract)". Everything I could not verify is listed under Gaps.

## KQ1. "Lazy Prices" (Cohen, Malloy, Nguyen): what is the signal, which sections matter, what alpha, what has happened since?

### Takeaway
The signal is year-over-year textual similarity of a firm's 10-K/10-Q (cosine, Jaccard, edit distance, simple diff all work); low-similarity "changers" underperform high-similarity "non-changers" by roughly 30–60 bp/month overall (1995–2014) and up to 188 bp/month when the change is concentrated in Item 1A Risk Factors, with no filing-date announcement effect and drift accruing over subsequent months. Post-publication, open replications on large caps (S&P 100, 2009–2026) find essentially zero alpha; one S&P 500 replication (1993–2024) finds the top-similarity bin still has the best long-run performance but no clean monotonic pattern, so the effect is best treated as concentrated in small/low-attention stocks and the pre-2010 era.

### Cited Findings
- Publication: Cohen, Malloy & Nguyen, "Lazy Prices," Journal of Finance 75(3), 2020 (NBER WP 25084, 2018). — [Wiley JF](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jofi.12885); [NBER w25084](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25084/w25084.pdf); [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/25084.html)
- Signal construction: the paper uses the complete history of regular quarterly and annual filings (10-K and 10-Q) by US corporations and tests four off-the-shelf similarity measures — cosine similarity on bag-of-words term frequencies, Jaccard similarity, minimum edit distance, and a simple word-level diff — all of which give similar answers; firms are sorted each month into quintiles by year-on-year similarity and forward returns examined (per search extract). — [Ivey copy of paper](https://www.ivey.uwo.ca/media/3776677/cohen.pdf); [Lazy Prices replication README summarising the paper](https://github.com/iqueipopg/lazy-prices)
- Headline effect size: a portfolio that shorts "changers" and buys "non-changers" earns up to 188 bp/month in alpha (over 22%/yr); when changes are concentrated in the Risk Factors section the alpha is 188 bp/month, t = 2.76 (per search extract). — [Quantt write-up](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/lazy-prices-snowflake-cortex-ai); [Quantpedia summary of CMN](https://quantpedia.com/the-positive-similarity-of-company-filings-and-the-cross-section-of-stock-returns/)
- Baseline (full-document) magnitude: long non-changers / short changers, rebalanced as new 10-Ks arrive and held for a year, earned 30 to 60 bp/month abnormal return on the CRSP universe 1995–2014 (per replication README summarising the paper). — [iqueipopg/lazy-prices](https://github.com/iqueipopg/lazy-prices)
- Mechanism/timing: there is no announcement effect associated with changes; returns accrue only when information is later revealed through news, events or earnings, consistent with investor inattention (per search extract of the abstract). — [NBER w25084](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25084/w25084.pdf)
- What changes predict: changes to 10-Ks predict future earnings, profitability, future news announcements, and future firm-level bankruptcies (per search extract). — [Wiley JF](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jofi.12885)
- Qualitative flavour of changes: firms whose annual report is nearly a copy of last year's tend to be firms where nothing bad is happening; firms that rewrite large parts of the 10-K are usually disclosing bad news (per replication README). — [iqueipopg/lazy-prices](https://github.com/iqueipopg/lazy-prices)
- Post-publication replication #1 (null): S&P 100 (99 firms), 1,792 10-Ks FY2007–FY2025, portfolio period March 2009–September 2026; quintile long Q5 (least changed) / short Q1 (most changed), formed the month after filing, held 12 months, equal-weighted, 10 bp cost per leg: annual return −0.92% gross (−1.24% net), Sharpe −0.11 [95% CI −0.56, 0.38], FF5+MOM alpha −0.93% (t = −0.49); within-cohort rank correlation between similarity and 12-month forward return 0.035 (t = 1.14); across 54 variants (3 measures × 3 sections × 3 group sizes × 2 lags) the best Sharpe was 0.54 (cosine, Item 1A, deciles, 2-month lag) but the deflated Sharpe ratio was 0.13 (certification threshold 0.95). Author's caveats: survivorship bias (every quintile shows 2–8% annual alpha), ~19 stocks per leg, cosine similarity saturated near 1.0 for large caps (median 0.996), 23% of filings missing Item 7 and 8% missing Item 1A in extraction. — [iqueipopg/lazy-prices](https://github.com/iqueipopg/lazy-prices)
- Post-publication replication #2 (weakly supportive): S&P 500 10-Ks 1993–2024, cosine similarity on word-frequency vectors, five annual bins: Bin 5 (most similar) "demonstrated the strongest performance over the long term," Bins 2 and 4 strong, Bin 3 underperformed; the authors note the similarity–return relation is "not linearly straightforward." — [martifigueres/LAZY-PRICES-REPLICATION-AND-DASHBOARD](https://github.com/martifigueres/LAZY-PRICES-REPLICATION-AND-DASHBOARD)
- Practitioner replication #3 (10-Q stronger than 10-K): point-in-time S&P 500 universe; signals = cosine/Jaccard/Levenshtein similarity, Word2Vec/sentence-encoder distances, FinBERT and LM sentiment; sector-neutral optimisation; best combined Sharpe ≈ 1.5; "the strongest signals were coming from the 10-Q's distance-based features," i.e. quarterly comparisons beat annual. — [calebyung/NLP-10-K-10-Q-Alpha-Research](https://github.com/calebyung/NLP-10-K-10-Q-Alpha-Research)
- Independent assessment: a structured evidence review on GitHub graded the Lazy Prices return claim "INSUFFICIENT post-publication," noting the only post-publication large-cap test (S&P 100, 2009–2026) is null and that no long-only, large-cap, net-of-cost figures exist. — [tradepartner issue #663](https://github.com/josejuarez96/tradepartner/issues/663)
- Related follow-up: Padyšák (SSRN 2020), "The Positive Similarity of Company Filings and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns," reports that similarity of the *positive-word* language is the most profitable variant: the lowest positive-similarity decile outperforms the highest by 0.41%/month (~5%/yr) — note the sign is opposite to CMN (low similarity of positive language = good) (per Quantpedia extract). — [SSRN 3690461](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3690461); [Quantpedia strategy page](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/the-positive-similarity-of-company-filings-and-stock-returns)
- Discussant critique exists (Kent Daniel, 2016 discussion of CMN) covering measurement and interpretation, but the document could not be fetched. — [Daniel discussion PDF](https://www.kentdaniel.net/discuss/2016/CMN.pdf)

### Inferences
- Practical construction consistent with the paper: for each new 10-K (or 10-Q vs. same quarter prior year), compute cosine similarity of Item 1A (and separately full text / MD&A) against the prior-year filing; rank within the filing cohort; go long top-quintile similarity, short bottom quintile; enter the month after filing; hold 12 months (CMN) or 3 months (quarterly refresh). Weight the Risk-Factors-section change most heavily — that is where CMN's largest alpha sits.
- The null S&P 100 result and the saturation of cosine similarity (~0.996 median) imply bag-of-words similarity has almost no cross-sectional dispersion among large caps; any live implementation should use small/mid caps and/or section-level or embedding-based change measures.
- The absence of an announcement effect means a trader has weeks, not hours, to act; the cost is that the drift is realised only when later news arrives, so holding periods below ~6 months probably capture little.
- Strong-form decay is plausible given (a) publication in 2018–2020, (b) the machine-readership adaptation documented by Cao et al. (KQ3), and (c) the SEC's 2020 Item 105 rewrite which forced a one-time restructuring of Item 1A for all filers (see KQ2), mechanically creating "changers" in FY2020–21 filings unrelated to fundamentals.

### Gaps
- I could not read the JF paper itself, so EW vs VW results, exact quintile-by-quintile returns, the Legal Proceedings / Business / Notes section breakdown, the CEO/CFO turnover result, and the 10-Q-only subsample numbers are not verified here. The 188 bp Risk-Factors figure and the 30–60 bp baseline come from secondary extracts.
- No peer-reviewed post-2020 replication in CRSP-universe small caps was found; the only out-of-sample tests found are GitHub projects of varying rigour.
- Adosoglou, Lombardo & Pardalos (2021) (doc2vec vector representations of filings) is cited as a follow-up but I could not retrieve its numbers.

## KQ2. Risk-factor (Item 1A) changes: do new/removed/reworded risk factors predict returns, volatility or events?

### Takeaway
The best-documented investable result is on 10-Q risk-factor *updates*: firms that update Item 1A in a 10-Q have lower 3-month post-filing returns, with a long-no-update / short-update value-weighted strategy yielding ~3.5%/yr alpha (5.3%/yr if the short leg is restricted to "weak" updates that avoid firm-fundamentals language), 2006–2014. Annual-level evidence (Campbell et al. 2014; Kravet & Muslu 2013; Hope, Hu & Lu 2016; Gaulin) shows risk-factor length, specificity and the addition of individual risk factors predict volatility, forecast dispersion and future adverse events, but Beatty, Cheng & Zhang (2019) document a post-2008 decline in informativeness, and a small modern test (2014–2023 large caps) finds no return predictability from risk-factor tone.

### Cited Findings
- Filzen, McBrayer & Shannon (2016, working paper submitted to the SEC's S7-06-16 comment file), "Risk Factor Disclosures: Do Managers and Markets Speak the Same Language?": sample of 52,955 10-Q filings by 4,353 firms with quarters ending 2006–2014; 26.3% of filings contain a risk-factor update; mean 3-month buy-and-hold abnormal return after filing is −0.54% (median −0.89%); controlling for the filing-date reaction, PEAD and other factors, updates are negatively associated with returns in the three months after the 10-Q; a value-weighted calendar-time strategy long firms with no update in the prior 3 months / short firms with an update yields positive and significant alpha of 3.53% annualised, driven by short-portfolio monthly intercepts of −0.23% to −0.29%; restricting the short leg to "weak" updates (bottom three quartiles of Balakrishnan–Bartov fundamentals-word counts) gives a monthly hedged intercept of 0.43–0.44% (5.28% annualised), while "strong" updates show near-zero, insignificant post-filing drift (because the filing-date reaction is more complete). — [sec.gov comment file PDF](https://www.sec.gov/comments/s7-06-16/s70616-369.pdf)
- Filzen (2015), Accounting Horizons 29(4): firms with updates to the risk-factors section in quarterly reports have significantly lower future unexpected earnings and are more likely to experience future extreme negative earnings shocks; 28% of firms had a risk update over 2006–2010 (per search extract and FMS citation). — [Accounting Horizons](https://publications.aaahq.org/accounting-horizons/article-abstract/29/4/887/2223/The-Information-Content-of-Risk-Factor-Disclosures); [CLS Blue Sky summary](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2017/01/11/do-investors-understand-quarterly-risk-factor-reporting/)
- Campbell, Chen, Dhaliwal, Lu & Steele (2014), Review of Accounting Studies: Item 1A disclosures increase investors' risk perceptions (higher return volatility and beta) and reduce information asymmetry (lower bid-ask spread); there is a negative association between the amount of disclosed risk factors and abnormal returns around the 10-K filing date (per search extract). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228232319_The_Information_Content_of_Mandatory_Risk_Factor_Disclosures_in_Corporate_Filings)
- Kravet & Muslu (2013), Review of Accounting Studies 18(4): annual *increases* in textual risk disclosures in 10-Ks are associated with increased stock-return volatility and trading volume around and after the filing, and with more dispersed analyst forecast revisions (per search extract). — [SSRN 1736228](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1736228); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-013-9228-9)
- Hope, Hu & Lu (2016), Review of Accounting Studies 21(4): 1005–1045: a "Specificity" measure (proper names, locations, organisations, percentages, dollar amounts, times, dates in Item 1A) is positively and significantly associated with the (unsigned) market reaction to the 10-K filing; analysts assess fundamental risk better when disclosures are more specific (per search extract). — [SSRN 2749132](https://www.ssrn.com/abstract=2749132); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-016-9371-1); [Kellogg Insight summary](https://insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu/article/investors-and-sec-risk-disclosure)
- Gaulin, "Risk Fact or Fiction: The Information Content of Risk Factor Disclosures" (Rice University dissertation / working paper): managers time the identification of new risk factors and the removal of old ones to align with the expected occurrence of future adverse outcomes; information is conveyed through the evolution of individual risk factors rather than total Item 1A length; results are inconsistent with pure copy-and-paste boilerplate (per search extract). — [Rice repository](https://repository.rice.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f00b7b25-82b8-4536-94f0-ed596a9a4050/content); [author page](https://mgaulin.com/research/risk-fact-or-fiction-the-information-content-of-risk-factor-disclosures.html)
- Beatty, Cheng & Zhang (2019), Contemporary Accounting Research, "Are Risk Factor Disclosures Still Relevant?": document a decrease in the relevance of risk discussion after the 2008 financial crisis and a lower association between risk-factor disclosure and future realised operating risks; Beatty et al. (2015) and Dyer et al. (2017) find risk-factor disclosure accounts for a substantial share of the growth in 10-K length (per search extract). — [Wiley CAR](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1911-3846.12444)
- Israelsen (2014), "Tell It Like It Is: Disclosed Risks and Factor Portfolios": extracts disclosed risk types from 10-Ks, relates them to volatility and factor loadings; disclosed credit-market/constraint risks explain much of the variation in Fama–French factors (per search extract). — [SSRN 2504522](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2504522)
- Lopez-Lira (2019), "Risk Factors That Matter: Textual Analysis of Risk Disclosures for the Cross-Section of Returns": unsupervised ML (LDA) on Item 1A identifies interpretable risk types; builds a 4-factor model whose portfolios are priced in the cross-section and contain information beyond standard multifactor models (per search extract). — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/Risk_Factors_that_matter_v4_1-29.pdf); [Mack Institute summary](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/2019/using-a-companys-own-words-to-assess-its-risks/)
- Huang, Ma & You (2024), "Expected Returns Based on Corporate Risk Disclosure": BERTopic on Item 1A yields risk-exposure vectors; peers defined by exposure-vector proximity explain more variation in returns, valuations and profitability than industry classifications; the risk-based expected-return proxy outperforms characteristics-based proxies in correlation with future returns and cross-sectional measurement-error variance (per search extract). — [Columbia PDF](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/imce-uploads/ADP/Huang%20Ma%20You%202024.pdf)
- Bao & Datta (2014), Management Science 60(6): 1371–1391: topic model that simultaneously discovers and quantifies risk types from Item 1A and relates them to stock-return volatility (per search extract). — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2014.1930)
- Negative modern evidence on *tone* of Item 1A: 376 filing-years, 41 large-cap US firms, FY2014–2023; FinBERT vs LM negative-word fraction (r = 0.57); 0 of 25 return-predictive regressions significant at 5%; tercile spreads all |t| < 1.9; raw volatility relation (most-negative tercile realised vol 0.31 vs 0.24, t ≈ 3.5) disappears after controlling for trailing 63-day vol (LM p ≈ 0.08, FinBERT p ≈ 0.54). — [ZacharyChai/finbert-10k-sentiment](https://github.com/ZacharyChai/finbert-10k-sentiment)
- Regime change to account for: SEC Release 33-10825 (effective November 9, 2020) amended Item 105 to require a summary risk-factor section if Item 1A exceeds 15 pages, replaced the "most significant" standard with "material," and required generic risks to be placed under a separate "General Risk Factors" heading at the end of the section. — [SEC final rule PDF](https://www.sec.gov/rules/final/2020/33-10825.pdf)
- Judicial/regulatory view of boilerplate: Cazier, McMullin & Treu (SSRN 3167611) examine whether lengthy and boilerplate risk-factor language is assessed as inadequate by courts and regulators. — [SSRN](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3167611)
- Review article: Isiaka (2021), Accounting Perspectives, "Risk Factor Disclosures: A Review and Directions for Future Research." — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1911-3838.12278)

### Inferences
- The most testable thesis is the 10-Q one: screen each 10-Q for whether Item 1A contains anything other than "no material changes" boilerplate; treat an update as a 3-month short/underweight signal, with extra weight on updates that are vague (few firm-fundamentals words), because the market under-reacts most to those. The sample ends in 2014 and the paper is unpublished, so this needs re-testing 2015–2025.
- Annual risk-factor *length* is a volatility signal, not a return signal: Campbell et al. and Kravet–Muslu link it to subsequent realised vol and forecast dispersion, which suggests an options/vol-selling use (long vol in firms with sharply expanded Item 1A) rather than a directional equity bet.
- Gaulin's result implies the atomic unit should be the individual risk factor (added/removed/reworded headings), not the section word count; this is also what CMN's section-level result points to.
- The November 2020 Item 105 rewrite means FY2020 and FY2021 similarity scores are contaminated by mandated restructuring; any backtest must either exclude that cohort or compare within-cohort ranks only.

### Gaps
- Could not obtain Campbell et al. (2014) or Kravet–Muslu coefficient magnitudes, or Gaulin's odds ratios for adverse events.
- No published paper found that directly measures returns to *removed* risk factors.
- No 2020–2026 peer-reviewed paper found that re-tests the 10-Q risk-factor-update return drift after 2014.
- Lopez-Lira's and Huang–Ma–You's portfolio alphas/t-stats could not be read.

## KQ3. Tone / sentiment (Loughran–McDonald lists, ML dictionaries, FinBERT, LLM sentiment, "Bloated Disclosures")

### Takeaway
LM negative tone robustly explains the 10-K filing-window (day 0 to +3) return and replicates (t ≈ −3 on ~51,000 10-Ks 1994–2008), but it is a contemporaneous-reaction measure, and machine-learning sentiment (Frankel–Jennings–Lee 2022) shows the dictionary's explanatory power is confined to the original LM sample period while ML measures continue to work. Firms have adapted: after the 2011 LM publication, high-machine-readership firms cut LM-negative words (Cao et al., RFS 2023), which is direct evidence of signal decay from crowding. GPT summaries of MD&A ("bloat") are shorter by >70% and explain filing reactions better than raw text, with bloated disclosures linked to adverse capital-market outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- Loughran & McDonald (2011), Journal of Finance 66(1), "When Is a Liability Not a Liability?": built finance-specific negative, positive, uncertainty, litigious, strong-modal and weak-modal lists from 10-Ks; almost three-fourths of Harvard-dictionary "negative" words are not negative in financial context; negative-tone score is negatively associated with filing-period excess returns and linked to trading volume, return volatility, fraud, material weaknesses and unexpected earnings (per search extract). — [SSRN 1331573](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1331573); [UTS copy of JF paper](https://www.uts.edu.au/globalassets/sites/default/files/adg_cons2015_loughran-mcdonald-je-2011.pdf); [LM publications page](https://sites.nd.edu/tim-loughran/publications/)
- Replication (open code): 50,902 firm-years, 8,937 firms, 1994–2008, 10-K/10-K405 only, Fama–MacBeth quarterly regressions; Fin-Neg proportional t = −2.99 (original −2.64); tf-idf-weighted Fin-Neg t = −2.69 (original −3.11); all coefficients negative; R² 2.35–2.58% vs original 2.45–2.76%; MD&A-section tone shows stronger effects than full filing. — [m4a1ak471994/lm2011-replication](https://github.com/m4a1ak471994/lm2011-replication)
- Jegadeesh & Wu (2013), Journal of Financial Economics 110(3): 712–729, "Word Power": term weighting learned from market reactions; significant relation between 10-K tone and market reaction for both negative and positive words; weighting matters at least as much as the word list itself (per search extract). — [SSRN 1787273](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1787273); [UPenn repository PDF](https://repository.upenn.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/caf9d0c9-0de5-475a-ac72-f6e4ad3bfce5/content)
- Frankel, Jennings & Lee (2022), Management Science 68(7): 5514–5532, "Disclosure Sentiment: Machine Learning vs. Dictionary Methods": ML (random-forest regression tree best) sentiment explains 10-K filing-date returns, whereas dictionary measures explain filing-date returns only during the original LM study period (per search extract). — [WashU profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/disclosure-sentiment-machine-learning-vs-dictionary-methods/); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v68y2022i7p5514-5532.html)
- García, Hu & Rohrer, "The colour of finance words" (JFE 2023; working paper dated 2022-11-08): ML-trained finance dictionary from 10-K filing returns (details not retrievable). — [Leeds working paper PDF](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/garcia/finsentiment20221108.pdf)
- Cao, Jiang, Yang & Zhang (2023), Review of Financial Studies 36(9): 3603–3642 (NBER WP 27950), "How to Talk When a Machine Is Listening": firms with high expected machine downloads of their filings avoid LM-negative words — but only after 2011 (publication of the LM dictionary) — with no such change for Harvard-dictionary negatives; such firms also exhibit more positive/excited speech emotion in calls (per search extract). — [NBER w27950](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w27950/w27950.pdf); [RePEc RFS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v36y2023i9p3603-3642..html); [CLS Blue Sky summary](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2020/10/01/how-to-talk-when-a-machine-is-listening-corporate-disclosure-in-the-age-of-ai/)
- Kim, Muhn & Nikolaev (2023), "Bloated Disclosures: Can ChatGPT Help Investors Process Information?" (Chicago Booth / BFI WP 2023-59; arXiv 2306.10224): GPT summaries of MD&A and earnings calls are >70% shorter yet have amplified information content; summary sentiment explains stock reactions better than raw-text sentiment; "bloat" (uninformative content share) is associated with adverse capital-market outcomes (per search extracts). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.10224v1); [BFI summary](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/insight/research-summary/bloated-disclosures-can-chatgpt-help-investors-process-information/); [BFI WP PDF mirror](https://smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/BFI_WP_2023-59.pdf)
- Index-level LLM tone: "Decoding risk sentiment in 10-K filings: Predictability for U.S. stock indices" (Finance Research Letters, 2025): 21,421 10-Ks 2002–2024; five Item 1A tone indicators (LM dictionary and GPT-3.5/4/4o/4o-mini calibrated) predict *weekly index returns*, optimism associated with higher returns, GPT-4 tone the most accurate (per search extract). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1544612325007317)
- FinBERT vs LM on Item 1A (2014–2023 large caps): no incremental predictive power of either for forward returns (see KQ2). — [ZacharyChai/finbert-10k-sentiment](https://github.com/ZacharyChai/finbert-10k-sentiment)
- Feldman, Govindaraj, Livnat & Segal (2010), Review of Accounting Studies 15(4): 915–953: *change* in MD&A tone adds to drift returns from 2 days after the SEC filing date through 1 day after the next quarter's preliminary earnings announcement, incremental to accruals and earnings surprises (per search extract). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/314899519_Management's_Tone_Change_Post_Earnings_Announcement_Drift_and_Accruals); [Bar-Ilan record](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/publications/managements-tone-change-post-earnings-announcement-drift-and-accr/)

### Inferences
- Level of LM negative tone is a filing-window reaction variable (R² ≈ 2.5%), not a drift signal; the investable versions are (a) *change* in tone vs prior filing (Feldman et al.), held until the next earnings announcement, and (b) ML-weighted tone (Jegadeesh–Wu / Frankel et al.) which has not visibly decayed the way the raw dictionary has.
- Cao et al. plus Frankel et al. together are the clearest evidence of crowding/decay in any 10-K text signal: once a lexicon is public, the issuers optimise against it. Any dictionary-based thesis should therefore be benchmarked on post-2011 data only.
- The "bloat" result suggests a thesis in which an LLM compresses MD&A and the *ratio* of informative to total length is the signal (high bloat = worse information environment); the paper's evidence is about reactions and information asymmetry, not a drift strategy.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve effect sizes for Kim–Muhn–Nikolaev (bloat coefficients), Cao et al. (percentage drop in negative words), García–Hu–Rohrer, or Frankel–Jennings–Lee (R² gains).
- No peer-reviewed evidence found that FinBERT or LLM *firm-level* sentiment of 10-K text predicts post-filing drift beyond LM; the FRL 2025 paper is index-level and weekly, which is a different (and more fragile) claim.

## KQ4. Readability / complexity / obfuscation (Fog, file size, Bog, firm-complexity words)

### Takeaway
Li (2008) linked harder-to-read 10-Ks to lower and less persistent earnings; Loughran & McDonald (2014, JF) showed the Fog index mis-measures financial readability and that 10-K file size (bytes or words) is a better proxy, predicting higher return volatility, analyst forecast errors and dispersion; Bonsall et al. (2017) offer the Bog index; LM's later "firm complexity" word list (JFQA 2023) predicts difficulty in projecting operations. The evidence is mostly about volatility and earnings quality, not directional return drift.

### Cited Findings
- Li (2008), Journal of Accounting and Economics: 10-Ks of firms with lower earnings are harder to read (Fog, length), and firms with easier-to-read 10-Ks have more persistent positive earnings (per search extract). — [cited in Loughran–McDonald 2014 summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228259498_Measuring_Readability_in_Financial_Disclosures)
- Loughran & McDonald (2014), Journal of Finance, "Measuring Readability in Financial Disclosures": the Fog index's word-complexity definition misclassifies readable financial documents; 10-K file size (text bytes or word count) is significantly associated with higher subsequent return volatility, earnings forecast errors and forecast dispersion; file size correlates 0.209 with Fog and 0.370 with Bog (per search extract). — [Wiley JF](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jofi.12162); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228259498_Measuring_Readability_in_Financial_Disclosures)
- Loughran & McDonald (2016 survey, JAR 54(4)): file size of annual reports predicts subsequent stock-return volatility and absolute earnings surprises (per search extract). — [Wiley JAR](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1475-679X.12123)
- Bonsall, Leone, Miller & Rennekamp (2017), JAE 63(2–3): 329–357: Bog index (Sentence Bog + Word Bog − Pep) captures plain-English attributes; validated with experiments and the SEC plain-English prospectus intervention; firm-level Bog data for 10-Ks are publicly posted. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jaecon/v63y2017i2p329-357.html); [Bog Index data](https://host.kelley.iu.edu/bpm/activities/bogindex.html)
- Loughran & McDonald, "Measuring Firm Complexity" (JFQA, 2023): a 374-word complexity list reduced by penalised regression to 53 words that consistently represent difficulty in projecting a firm's future operations; the measure plus 10-K size "dominates traditional measures" of firm complexity (per search extract). — [Cambridge PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/D737FD0A697AF699C5AADD62842ACAB8/S0022109023000716a.pdf/measuring-firm-complexity.pdf); [SRAF complexity page](https://sraf.nd.edu/complexity/)
- Dyer, Lang & Stice-Lawrence (2017), JAE 64(2–3): 221–245: 1996–2013 10-Ks show rising length, boilerplate, stickiness and redundancy and falling specificity, readability and share of hard information; new FASB/SEC requirements explain most of the length increase, with 3 of 150 LDA topics — fair value, internal controls, risk factors — accounting for virtually all of it. — [SSRN 2741682](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2741682); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jaecon/v64y2017i2p221-245.html)
- Filzen & Peterson (2015), Contemporary Accounting Research, "Financial Statement Complexity and Meeting Analysts' Expectations" (complexity and earnings-expectation outcomes; details not retrieved). — [Wiley CAR](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1911-3846.12135)
- Bushee, Gow & Taylor (2018), Journal of Accounting Research, "Linguistic Complexity in Firm Disclosures: Obfuscation or Information?" — not retrievable in this session; listed as the key paper decomposing complexity into obfuscation vs information components.

### Inferences
- The investable use of readability is as a *volatility / earnings-surprise* conditioner: sort on 10-K file size (or complexity-word share) controlling for industry and size, and expect wider earnings-surprise distributions and higher realised vol in the top decile; this supports options-based or risk-budgeting theses more than long-short equity.
- Because regulation drove most of the length growth (Dyer et al.), raw length comparisons across years are confounded; use within-year cross-sectional ranks or industry-adjusted file size.

### Gaps
- No paper found with a clean long-short *return* alpha from readability alone; Li (2008)'s earnings-persistence result is the closest to a return thesis (via a persistence-mispricing channel) but its return tests were not retrievable.
- Bushee–Gow–Taylor findings not verified.

## KQ5. Boilerplate vs specific language, forward-looking statements, uncertainty/hedging, litigation language, going-concern language, Critical Audit Matters

### Takeaway
Specificity (named entities, numbers) earns stronger filing reactions (Hope–Hu–Lu); non-forecast forward-looking statements in MD&A move prices and analysts (Muslu et al. 2015; Bozanic et al. 2018); MD&A going-concern language plus tone predicts bankruptcy up to three years ahead, incremental to ratios and the auditor's opinion (Mayew et al. 2015, TAR); CAMs (post-2019) show mixed evidence — more/longer CAMs are associated with more negative reactions, higher volatility and wider forecast dispersion (Klevak et al. 2023), which the authors interpret as possible misinterpretation rather than information.

### Cited Findings
- Specificity: Hope, Hu & Lu (2016) — see KQ2 — market reaction to the 10-K is positively associated with Item 1A specificity. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-016-9371-1)
- Boilerplate trend: Dyer, Lang & Stice-Lawrence (2017) document rising boilerplate and stickiness 1996–2013. — [SSRN 2741682](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2741682)
- Forward-looking statements: Muslu, Radhakrishnan, Subramanyam & Lim (2015), Management Science, "Forward-Looking MD&A Disclosures and the Information Environment": Naïve-Bayes classifier on 10-K and 10-Q MD&A; non-forecast FLS generate significant investor and analyst responses; firms with poor information environments provide more FLS, helping investors anticipate future earnings (per search extract). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/276482099_Forward-Looking_MDA_Disclosures_and_the_Information_Environment)
- Bozanic, Roulstone & Van Buskirk (2018), JAE 65(1): 1–20, "Management Earnings Forecasts and Other Forward-Looking Statements": separates forecast-like from other FLS (per search extract). — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jaecon/v65y2018i1p1-20.html); [NYU Stern PDF](https://www.stern.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/assets/documents/Darren%20Roulstone_51415.pdf)
- Uncertainty / litigious / modal words: the LM (2011) uncertainty, litigious and modal lists are linked to filing returns, volume and volatility (per search extract of LM 2011/2016). — [LM 2011 SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1331573)
- Going concern in MD&A: Mayew, Sethuraman & Venkatachalam (2015), The Accounting Review 90(4): 1621–1651, "MD&A Disclosure and the Firm's Ability to Continue as a Going Concern": bankrupt-firm sample 1995–2012; management's going-concern opinion in MD&A and MD&A linguistic tone together have significant explanatory power for subsequent bankruptcy, incremental to financial ratios, market variables and the auditor's going-concern opinion, extending to three years before bankruptcy (per search extract). — [Lopatta et al. 2017 citing Mayew et al.](https://doi.org/10.1111/1911-3838.12150)
- 10-K narrative bankruptcy prediction (2026): "Bankruptcy Prediction from 10-K Narratives: Evidence from Interpretable Text Scores and Accounting Baselines" (Risks 14(8):179, 2026; arXiv 2606.05623) — tests interpretable text scores vs accounting baselines; numbers not retrievable. — [MDPI DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/risks14080179); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.05623)
- Credit events: Donovan, Jennings, Koharki & Lee (2021), Review of Accounting Studies, "Measuring credit risk using qualitative disclosure": ML measure from MD&A and conference-call text improves out-of-sample prediction of bankruptcies, interest spreads and rating downgrades relative to existing credit-risk measures (per search extract). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-020-09575-4); [BYU record](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/8489/)
- CAMs — Klevak, Livnat, Pei & Suslava, "Critical Audit Matters: Possible Market Misinterpretation," Auditing: A Journal of Practice & Theory 42(3): 45–70 (2023), first-wave CAM filers July 2019–May 2020: market reactions are more negative for firms with more CAM disclosures, analysts cut forecasts more, stock prices become more volatile and forecast dispersion rises; the authors frame this as investors possibly misreading audit complexity as company uncertainty (per search extract). Earlier version "Are Critical Audit Matters Informative?" (NYU Stern WP 2020; QMA/PGIM authors). — [Bucknell record](https://digitalcommons.bucknell.edu/fac_journ/1966/); [Warwick WRAP](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/171996/); [PCAOB conference PDF](https://pcaob-assets.azureedge.net/pcaob-dev/docs/default-source/economicandriskanalysis/conference/documents/26---are-critical-audit-matters-informative.pdf)
- CAMs — other evidence (per search extracts): some studies find CAM firms have higher earnings response coefficients and abnormal volume and improved value relevance of earnings; others find minimal short-window reaction and that multiple CAMs add cost without informativeness; an M&A-announcement study (Abbott & Buslepp, SSRN 4188255) asks whether CAMs are decision-relevant. — [Abbott & Buslepp SSRN](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4188255); [Costs and benefits of CAMs, JAPP](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0882611022000608); [Unintended Effects of CAMs](https://publications.aaahq.org/cia/article/19/1/P1/12934/Unintended-Effects-of-Critical-Audit-Matters-Early); [Burke et al., Disclosure and Consequences of US CAMs](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362988679_The_Disclosure_and_Consequences_of_US_Critical_Audit_Matters); [When CAMs are Informative (Bretton Woods conf.)](https://www.brettonwoodsskiconference.com/uploads/b/f9bfc8b0-0251-11ed-a646-3dea17112d2f/When%20Critical%20Audit%20Matters%20are%20Informative.pdf)
- Item 3 Legal Proceedings: no academic paper specifically on Item 3 text predicting returns was found; CMN's section-level analysis includes Legal Proceedings (number not retrieved).

### Inferences
- A going-concern thesis is well supported for shorts/credit: flag MD&A text containing going-concern / substantial-doubt language and negative-tone shifts in the MD&A up to three years before distress, using Mayew et al. as the template; combine with Donovan et al.-style ML credit scores.
- CAMs: the consistent finding is a *volatility/dispersion* response to more or longer CAMs, not a return drift; a tradable version is to go long realised-vol exposure around earnings for firms with new or expanded CAMs. The "misinterpretation" framing suggests any negative filing-window reaction may reverse — an untested contrarian idea.
- Forward-looking statement counts are an information-environment variable; a change in the number or tone of non-forecast FLS vs the prior 10-K/10-Q is a plausible earnings-direction signal but no drift alpha was found in the literature retrieved.

### Gaps
- No evidence located on Item 3 text specifically, on hedging-language changes as a return signal, or on auditor-report going-concern *wording* (vs the opinion itself) predicting returns (Kausar–Taffler–Tan "going-concern anomaly" could not be retrieved because the search budget was exhausted).
- Rogers, Van Buskirk & Zechman (2011) on optimistic tone and litigation was not retrievable.
- CAM evidence is all 2019–2022 first-wave; no study found on CAM *changes* year-over-year (new CAM added/removed) as a signal.

## KQ6. MD&A: year-over-year modification, tone and future performance, deceptive language / fraud and misstatement

### Takeaway
Brown & Tucker (2011, JAR) show investors react more to 10-Ks whose MD&A is modified more (cosine-based modification score), analysts do not, and firms with larger economic changes modify more; Feldman et al. (2010) show MD&A *tone change* predicts drift to the next earnings announcement; the fraud literature (Hoberg & Lewis 2017, Purda & Skillicorn 2015, Larcker & Zakolyukina 2012 for calls) finds fraud firms' MD&A under-reports detail and overstates performance/growth, with topic/ML models achieving materially better detection than ratios alone.

### Cited Findings
- Brown & Tucker (2011), Journal of Accounting Research (May 2011), "Large-Sample Evidence on Firms' Year-over-Year MD&A Modifications": introduces a cosine-based modification score; stock-price response to 10-K filings is positively associated with the MD&A modification score; analyst forecast revisions are not; firms with larger economic changes modify more; the MD&A contains new information for predicting future cash flows but little about next-period earnings (per search extracts). — [Wiley JAR](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1475-679X.2010.00396.x); [UF PDF](http://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/tucker/2011_Brown_and_Tucker_MDA.pdf); [author page](https://www.stephenvbrown.com/publications/papers/annual-mda-modifications/)
- Feldman, Govindaraj, Livnat & Segal (2010), RAST 15(4): MD&A tone change adds to drift returns from filing+2 days to one day after the next quarter's earnings release, incremental to accruals and SUE. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/314899519_Management's_Tone_Change_Post_Earnings_Announcement_Drift_and_Accruals)
- Hoberg & Lewis (2017), Journal of Corporate Finance 43: 58–85, "Do Fraudulent Firms Produce Abnormal Disclosure?": LDA topic modelling of MD&A; fraudulent firms produce verbal disclosure that is abnormal relative to strong benchmarks; fraud firms under-report detail and overstate performance and growth potential (per search extracts). — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/corfin/v43y2017icp58-85.html); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/312450203_Do_fraudulent_firms_produce_abnormal_disclosure)
- Purda & Skillicorn (2015), Contemporary Accounting Research, "Accounting Variables, Deception, and a Bag of Words": SVM on the 200 most predictive words from MD&A of annual and quarterly reports distinguishes fraudulent from truthful reports (per search extract). — [Queen's PDF](https://smith.queensu.ca/_templates/documents/governance/reports/accounting-variables.pdf)
- Larcker & Zakolyukina (2012), Journal of Accounting Research, "Detecting Deceptive Discussions in Conference Calls": dictionary of psychology/linguistics deception cues applied to call transcripts (not 10-K text) (per search extract). — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Detecting-Deceptive-Discussions-in-Conference-Calls-Larcker-Zakolyukina/11a045aaf95bd5cd5490e3d84533d7757e9632b5)
- Brown, Crowley & Elliott (2020), JAR, "What Are You Saying? Using Topic to Detect Financial Misreporting": topic-based detection of misreporting (cited alongside Hoberg–Lewis in extracts). — [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/3526/edfec08aa0c44808541d6a8a1b3ee55c81b6.pdf)
- Recent ML/LLM fraud work: "Accounting fraud detection using contextual language learning" (2024) and "Financial statement fraud detection using topic-driven financial sentiment analysis" (2026) extend this to transformer models (details not retrieved). — [ScienceDirect 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1467089524000150); [ScienceDirect 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167923626000047)

### Inferences
- MD&A modification is a *reaction* signal (investors respond at filing) whereas Risk-Factor modification (CMN) is a *drift* signal; a combined thesis would use MD&A tone change (Feldman) for the drift leg and MD&A topic abnormality (Hoberg–Lewis) as a fraud/short screen.
- The fraud literature's consistent markers — less detail, more growth/performance language, abnormal topic mix vs industry peers — translate to a screen: rank firms by distance of their MD&A topic vector from the industry-year centroid and by (performance+growth words)/(detail words).

### Gaps
- Brown & Tucker coefficient magnitudes and the time trend in modification scores were not retrievable.
- Detection accuracies (AUC) for Hoberg–Lewis and Purda–Skillicorn not verified.
- No study found converting MD&A fraud scores into a post-filing short-portfolio alpha.

## KQ7. Hoberg–Phillips text-based industries (Item 1) and product-market fluidity: investment uses

### Takeaway
TNIC peers built from Item 1 product descriptions define better competitor sets than SIC/NAICS; shocks to less-visible TNIC peers generate economically large momentum profits that exceed own-firm momentum, strongest where SIC and TNIC peers disagree (Hoberg & Phillips, JFQA 2018); product-market fluidity (threat from rivals' product changes) predicts lower payouts and higher cash holdings (JF 2014) and makes cash flows harder to forecast — a volatility/uncertainty signal rather than a direct return signal.

### Cited Findings
- Hoberg & Phillips, "Text-Based Industry Momentum" (JFQA 2018): TNIC peers identified from 10-K product text; shocks to less visible peers generate economically large momentum profits, stronger than own-firm momentum variables; shocks in product markets where SIC and TNIC peers disagree (high disparity) generate the strongest momentum returns (per search extract). — [Tuck PDF](https://faculty.tuck.dartmouth.edu/images/uploads/faculty/gordon-phillips/hoberg_phillips_TNICmomJFQA_FinV1.pdf)
- Hoberg, Phillips & Prabhala (2014), Journal of Finance, "Product Market Threats, Payouts, and Financial Flexibility": product-market fluidity (changes in rivals' products relative to the firm's) decreases propensity to pay dividends and repurchase and increases cash holdings; when fluidity is very high, future cash flows are hard to predict (per search extracts). — [Wiley JF](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jofi.12050); [SSRN 1787315](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1787315)
- Data: TNIC, fluidity and related measures are distributed via the Hoberg–Phillips Data Library (fluidity readme). — [Fluidity readme](https://hobergphillips.tuck.dartmouth.edu/idata/Readme_FluidityData.txt)
- Related: "Stock Return Dependence and Product Market Linkages" (AEA 2018) and "The Bright Side of Product Market Threats: The Case of Innovation" use the same text-based competition measures (per search listing). — [AEA paper](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2018/preliminary/paper/dGQ3hGSZ); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1059056020302112)
- Huang, Ma & You (2024) show risk-disclosure-based peers (from Item 1A) explain more return/valuation variation than industry classifications — a complement to Item 1-based TNIC. — [Columbia PDF](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/imce-uploads/ADP/Huang%20Ma%20You%202024.pdf)

### Inferences
- Concrete thesis: TNIC-peer momentum — each month, compute the lagged return of a firm's TNIC peers (especially those not sharing its SIC code) and go long firms whose text-peers have rallied / short those whose text-peers have fallen; the paper's "disparity" result says to overweight firms where TNIC and SIC peer sets diverge.
- Fluidity is a natural conditioning variable for Lazy Prices-type tests: high-fluidity firms *should* rewrite their Business/Risk sections, so textual change there is less informative than the same change at a low-fluidity firm.

### Gaps
- Could not read the JFQA paper, so the monthly return spread and t-stats for text-based industry momentum are not verified.
- No post-2018 replication of TNIC momentum found.

## KQ8. 10-Q (quarterly) text changes vs 10-K

### Takeaway
Quarterly evidence is thinner but, where tested, stronger per unit of time: 10-Q risk-factor updates predict negative 3-month drift (FMS 2016: 3.5–5.3%/yr alpha, 2006–2014) and lower future unexpected earnings (Filzen 2015); CMN's Lazy Prices uses both 10-K and 10-Q filings; one practitioner backtest found 10-Q distance features to be the strongest signals; Padyšák's positive-similarity result also uses 10-K and 10-Q.

### Cited Findings
- Quarterly updates are legally different: 10-Qs need only report *changes* to risk factors, so an update is by construction new information; the requirement began for quarters in fiscal years ending after December 1, 2006. — [FMS 2016 PDF on sec.gov](https://www.sec.gov/comments/s7-06-16/s70616-369.pdf)
- FMS (2016): 52,955 10-Qs, 2006–2014, 26.3% with updates; 3-month BHAR lower for updaters; L/S alpha 3.53%/yr (5.28% for weak updates). — [sec.gov PDF](https://www.sec.gov/comments/s7-06-16/s70616-369.pdf)
- Filzen (2015): 10-Q risk updates predict lower future unexpected earnings and more extreme negative earnings shocks. — [Accounting Horizons](https://publications.aaahq.org/accounting-horizons/article-abstract/29/4/887/2223/The-Information-Content-of-Risk-Factor-Disclosures)
- CMN (2020) use "regular quarterly and annual filings" and the trading strategy shorts firms with large changes in 10-K and 10-Q reports (per search extracts). — [Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/the-positive-similarity-of-company-filings-and-the-cross-section-of-stock-returns/)
- Practitioner backtest: "the strongest signals were coming from the 10-Q's distance-based features," S&P 500 point-in-time universe. — [calebyung/NLP-10-K-10-Q-Alpha-Research](https://github.com/calebyung/NLP-10-K-10-Q-Alpha-Research)
- Muslu et al. (2015) classify FLS in both 10-K and 10-Q MD&A. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/276482099_Forward-Looking_MDA_Disclosures_and_the_Information_Environment)

### Inferences
- For 10-Q similarity, the correct comparison is same-quarter-prior-year (seasonality in MD&A) for MD&A, but prior-quarter for Item 1A updates (which are cumulative "changes since the 10-K").
- Quarterly refresh gives four signals a year and a 3-month horizon, which matters for capacity and decay testing.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found that reports Lazy Prices-style similarity alphas separately for 10-Qs.
- The FMS working paper does not appear to have been published; no post-2014 re-test found.

## KQ9. 2023–2026 LLM-based re-examinations: do LLM measures improve on bag-of-words?

### Takeaway
Evidence that LLMs beat bag-of-words is strongest for *news* and *earnings calls* (Chen–Kelly–Xiu; Lopez-Lira–Tang; "From Text to Alpha" on S&P 100 calls 2010–2024 with >2× risk-adjusted alpha vs an NER baseline) and for structured financial-statement prediction (Kim–Muhn–Nikolaev 2024: GPT-4 beats analysts directionally). For 10-K narrative specifically, the published gains are in explaining filing reactions (bloat summaries), index-level weekly predictability (GPT-4 Item 1A tone), better peer/exposure measurement (BERTopic on Item 1A), and taxonomy-aligned risk extraction; a small firm-level test finds FinBERT adds nothing over LM for forward returns. Look-ahead bias in pretrained models is a live methodological concern.

### Cited Findings
- Chen, Kelly & Xiu, "Expected Returns and Large Language Models" (SSRN 4416687): LLM embeddings (ChatGPT, LLaMA) of financial *news* contain incremental return information beyond reversal and characteristics and substantially outperform bag-of-words and Word2Vec; portfolios earn economically meaningful Sharpe ratios after costs and are not driven by look-ahead bias (per search extract). — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4416687)
- Lopez-Lira & Tang (2023), "Can ChatGPT Forecast Stock Price Movements?": ChatGPT news-headline scores significantly predict next-day returns and beat traditional sentiment (per search extract). — [SSRN 4412788](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4412788); [arXiv 2304.07619](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07619)
- Choi, Kim, Yu, Golkhou, Halperin, Papaioannou, Kim, Wang, Kwon, Kim, Lopez-Lira & Lee, "From Text to Alpha: Can LLMs Track Evolving Signals in Corporate Disclosures?" (arXiv 2510.03195, 2025): "LLM as extractor, embedding as ruler" — extract metric-focused spans, measure semantic shift across consecutive disclosures ("moving targets"); >2× risk-adjusted alpha vs an NER-based baseline; data are S&P 100 *earnings calls* 2010–2024, not 10-Ks (per GitHub issue extract and search extract). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.03195); [GitHub issue summary](https://github.com/KatsuyaITO/indx-autoresearch-financeaiready/issues/1973)
- Kim, Muhn & Nikolaev (2024), "Financial Statement Analysis with Large Language Models" (arXiv 2407.17866): GPT-4 given anonymised, standardised statements (no narrative) predicts the direction of earnings changes better than analysts and on par with a trained ANN (per search extract). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17866)
- Kim, Muhn & Nikolaev (2023), "Bloated Disclosures" and "From Transcripts to Insights" (GPT-based risk measures from calls) — see KQ3. — [Tilburg copy of Transcripts paper](https://www.tilburguniversity.edu/sites/default/files/download/Kim,%20Muhn,%20Nikolaev%20-%20From%20Transcripts%20to%20Insights.pdf)
- "Decoding risk sentiment in 10-K filings" (FRL 2025): GPT-4-calibrated Item 1A tone on 21,421 10-Ks (2002–2024) predicts weekly US index returns better than LM (per search extract). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1544612325007317)
- Huang, Ma & You (2024): BERTopic risk-exposure vectors from Item 1A outperform characteristics-based expected-return proxies (per search extract). — [Columbia PDF](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/imce-uploads/ADP/Huang%20Ma%20You%202024.pdf)
- "Taxonomy-Aligned Risk Extraction from 10-K Filings with Autonomous Improvement Using LLMs" (arXiv 2601.15247, 2026) and "GRAB: A Risk Taxonomy-Grounded Benchmark for Unsupervised Topic Discovery in Financial Disclosures" (arXiv 2509.21698) — LLM risk extraction methods; no return results retrieved. — [arXiv 2601.15247](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.15247); [arXiv 2509.21698](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.21698)
- "Uncovering Financial Distress with Textual Risk Disclosures in Annual Reports: Insights from Large Language Models" (Information Systems Frontiers, 2025) — LLM on Item 1A for distress prediction; details not retrieved. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10637-8)
- "Utilizing Pre-trained and Large Language Models for 10-K Items Segmentation" (arXiv 2502.08875) — addresses the item-extraction failure that plagued the S&P 100 Lazy Prices replication (23% missing Item 7). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.08875)
- Negative firm-level result: FinBERT vs LM on Item 1A 2014–2023 — neither predicts forward abnormal returns; no incremental power. — [ZacharyChai/finbert-10k-sentiment](https://github.com/ZacharyChai/finbert-10k-sentiment)
- Methodological caution: "Chronologically Consistent Large Language Models" (arXiv 2502.21206) and Chen, Green, Gulen & Zhou, "What Does ChatGPT Make of Historical Stock Returns? Extrapolation and Miscalibration in LLM Stock Return Forecasts" (SSRN 4941906) address look-ahead/training-data contamination and LLM forecast miscalibration. — [arXiv 2502.21206](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.21206); [SSRN 4941906](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4941906)
- Practitioner: a Snowflake Cortex implementation of Lazy Prices with LLM embeddings exists (Quantt), page not retrievable. — [Quantt](https://www.quantt.co.uk/resources/lazy-prices-snowflake-cortex-ai)

### Inferences
- Where LLMs clearly add value for 10-K text is in *measurement*: segmenting items reliably, compressing MD&A to informative content (bloat), extracting individual risk factors into a taxonomy (so "new/removed risk factor" can be tracked at the atomic level Gaulin found informative), and building exposure vectors for peer definition. The return-prediction gains documented so far are mostly in other corpora (news, calls).
- The "moving targets" idea (which metrics management stops emphasising) is directly portable from calls to MD&A, and its >2× alpha vs NER baseline on calls is the most promising LLM-era signal to re-test on 10-K/10-Q MD&A.
- Any LLM-based backtest must use chronologically consistent models or anonymised inputs to avoid memorised outcomes.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found that reports a long-short alpha from LLM-extracted 10-K features that is benchmarked head-to-head against CMN-style cosine similarity on the same sample.
- "From Text to Alpha" absolute alphas, Sharpe and t-stats not retrieved; the paper's data are calls, not filings.
- No evidence retrieved on crowding/decay of LLM-based filing signals (too recent).
