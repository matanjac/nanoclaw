# Text in "Other" SEC Filings (S-1/424B4, DEF 14A, Comment Letters, Exhibits, Fund Filings, Small-Company and Deregistration Forms) as Predictors of Returns, Earnings and Corporate Events

Scope note: this file covers SEC forms OTHER than 10-K/10-Q narrative, 8-K, Form 4 and 13D/13G/13F. Access constraint: the research proxy blocked SSRN, Wiley, Springer, INFORMS, Federal Reserve, RePEc, eScholarship and several university PDF hosts, so most effect sizes below come from publisher/aggregator abstracts and search-engine summaries rather than full-text reads. Where a number could not be verified against the primary text, it is flagged. Two PDFs (the Wen-Zhu SPAC paper and the Robinhood comment-letter paper) were read in full text locally.

Ranking summary (by volume and quality of tradable, return-based evidence) — details and sources in the final section:
1. IPO registration statements (S-1 / 424B4) — multiple JFE/RFS papers with first-day-return, price-revision, volatility and post-IPO-return effects; tradable only through IPO allocation or first-day/after-market positioning.
2. SEC comment letters (UPLOAD / CORRESP) — The Accounting Review and RAST evidence of a 1–5% negative 50-day drift after public release, strongest for revenue-recognition letters; genuinely tradable post-disclosure drift with documented inattention.
3. Material contracts (EX-10 credit agreements) — causal evidence that newly imposed covenants are followed by higher market value/operating performance (Nini-Smith-Sufi); LLM extraction is now feasible but no published "covenant-text long/short" alpha.
4. Proxy statements (DEF 14A) — strong governance-outcome evidence (say-on-pay dissent, RPT valuation discounts, golden parachutes and negative abnormal returns), weaker direct return-prediction evidence.
5. Mutual fund filings (N-CSR letters, N-1A prospectuses) — robust flow prediction and some alpha prediction from prospectus risk-disclosure updates; applies to funds, not stocks.
6. De-SPAC proxies (DEF 14A / S-4) — one working paper linking overconfident text to larger post-listing price declines and lawsuits.
7. Deregistration (Form 15) — ~10% negative announcement return (event, not text).
8. S-3 shelf / ATM, 10-K/A, S-8, Form D, Reg A (1-A), Form C, 20-F/6-K, AAER/litigation releases — thin or no peer-reviewed text-based return evidence found.

## Key Question 1: IPO prospectuses (S-1, 424B4), follow-ons and SPAC S-4/proxy text

### Takeaway
S-1/424B4 text is the best-documented "other form" for return prediction: uncertain/negative/weak-modal tone and "standard" (boilerplate) content predict higher first-day returns, larger price revisions and higher post-IPO volatility (JFE 2013, RFS 2010), while informative content and clear risk-factor disclosure reduce underpricing; SEC pre-IPO comment letters about revenue recognition predict lower post-IPO returns (RFS 2020). De-SPAC proxies that are less pessimistic/more definitive than matched IPO S-1s are followed by larger share-price declines (working paper, 2022).

### Cited Findings
- Loughran & McDonald, "IPO First-Day Returns, Offer Price Revisions, Volatility, and Form S-1 Language," Journal of Financial Economics 109(2), Aug 2013, pp. 307–326: IPOs with high levels of uncertain text have higher first-day returns, larger absolute offer-price revisions, and higher subsequent volatility; a one-standard-deviation increase in the proportion of uncertain and negative language in the S-1 is associated with roughly 3% and 4% higher first-day returns respectively; the paper introduces six word lists (uncertain = 285 words, weak modal, negative, positive, legal, strong modal) — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256030728_IPO_First-Day_Returns_Offer_Price_Revisions_Volatility_and_Form_S-1_Language); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X13000603); [Loughran publications page](https://sites.nd.edu/tim-loughran/publications/)
- Hanley & Hoberg, "The Information Content of IPO Prospectuses," Review of Financial Studies 23(7), July 2010, pp. 2821–2864: decomposes 424B prospectus text into "standard" content (similar to prior prospectuses in the industry) and "informative" content; a one-standard-deviation increase in standard content raises underpricing by about 4%, and a one-standard-deviation increase in informative content lowers underpricing by about 8%; the four most informative sections are Prospectus Summary, Risk Factors, Use of Proceeds and MD&A — [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/40782968); [SCIRP citation record](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=2487677)
- Hanley & Hoberg, "Litigation Risk, Strategic Disclosure and the Underpricing of Initial Public Offerings," Journal of Financial Economics 2012 (FEDS working paper 2011-12): firms trade off risk-factor disclosure against underpricing as litigation insurance (full text blocked; only the FEDS listing was reachable) — [FEDS 2011-12](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2011/201112/index.html); [Lehigh copy](https://business.lehigh.edu/sites/default/files/2019-08/1%20hanley_hoberg_jfe_2012_2.pdf)
- Arnold, Fishe & North, "The Effects of Ambiguous Information on Initial and Subsequent IPO Returns," Financial Management 2010: measures ambiguity in the Risk Factors section as the ratio of soft to hard information word counts; more complete/informative risk-factor disclosure is associated with lower underpricing during and after the IPO — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227672049_The_Effects_of_Ambiguous_Information_on_Initial_and_Subsequent_IPO_Returns)
- Ferris, Hao & Liao, "The Effect of Issuer Conservatism on IPO Pricing and Performance," Review of Finance 2013: uses negative-word ratios as "conservatism"; more conservative prospectus language leads to higher underpricing — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/255728167_The_Effect_of_Issuer_Conservatism_on_IPO_Pricing_and_Performance)
- Ding, "Disclosure of Downside Risk and Investors' Use of Qualitative Information: Evidence from the IPO Prospectus's Risk Factor Section," International Review of Finance 2016 — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/irfi.12066)
- Lowry, Michaely & Volkova, "Information Revealed through the Regulatory Process: Interactions between the SEC and Companies ahead of Their IPO," Review of Financial Studies 33(12), 2020, pp. 5510–5554: uses LDA topics and KL divergence on pre-IPO SEC comment letters and responses; revenue recognition is the dominant SEC concern and is not independently discovered by investors; greater SEC concern about revenue recognition is associated with more secondary sales, lower post-IPO liquidity, lower post-IPO returns and a higher probability of withdrawal — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/345359668_Information_Revealed_through_the_Regulatory_Process_Interactions_between_the_SEC_and_Companies_ahead_of_Their_IPO); [RePEc listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v33y2020i12p5510-5554..html); [author page](http://www.evolkova.info/research/sec_letters/)
- Wen & Zhu, "Is Going Public via SPAC Regulatory Arbitrage? A Textual Analysis Approach," working paper dated March 25, 2022 (filed as SEC comment letter S7-13-22 with D. Nandy): comparing de-SPAC DEF 14A / S-4 proxy text to propensity-matched IPO S-1s, de-SPAC filings contain 44% fewer pessimistic statements, 59% more definitive statements and 56% fewer unique financial words; de-SPAC firms experience a higher incidence of securities class actions and a larger decline in share prices after going public; differences vanish in the first 10-K; only 48% of de-SPAC transactions received any SEC comment letter vs mandatory review of IPOs; 2015–2020 sample: 112 of 573 (19.5%) conventional IPOs and 23 (20%) of de-SPAC firms were sued; Loughran-McDonald word-list proportions are used (%Litigious, %StrongModal, %Constraining, %Positive, %Negative, %Uncertain) — [SEC comment file PDF (full text read locally)](https://www.sec.gov/comments/s7-13-22/s71322-20130553-299411.pdf)
- A 2022 FinNLP paper "Prospectus Language and IPO Performance" applies modern NLP to prospectus text (full text blocked) — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2022.finnlp-1.21.pdf)
- A 2025 Journal of Banking & Finance paper, "Predicting IPO first-day returns: Evidence from machine learning analyses," finds XGBoost the most effective model using 863 pre-IPO variables; the most influential predictors are underwriter agency measures, price revision and free-float fraction (not text) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378426625001207)
- A 2019–2024 US IPO measurement study combining prospectus text, deal terms, underwriter reputation and market regime concluded first-day returns are not forecastable from pre-trade observables; the one text effect that survived multiple-testing correction (more legal language → lower first-day return) disappeared after controlling for deal size, offer price and market regime — [GitHub repo summary](https://github.com/leeyawnnn/ipo_underpricing_model) (non-peer-reviewed)
- "Measuring misleading information in IPO prospectuses," Review of Quantitative Finance and Accounting 2021 — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11156-021-00964-7)
- "Soft Strategic Information and IPO Underpricing," Journal of Behavioral Finance 17(1), 2016 — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15427560.2016.1133619)

### Inferences
- Construction for a first-day-return thesis: score the final S-1/A or 424B4 (ideally only the Summary, Risk Factors, Use of Proceeds and MD&A sections per Hanley-Hoberg) for L&M uncertain + negative + weak-modal word proportions and for cosine similarity to same-industry prior prospectuses (standard content). High-uncertainty / high-standard-content issues are expected to be underpriced more (positive first-day return but also larger price-revision and volatility) — the tradable expression is IPO allocation demand or first-day long positions, not secondary-market alpha.
- The Lowry-Michaely-Volkova result is the most directly "short-able" signal: count revenue-recognition topics in the pre-IPO UPLOAD/CORRESP exchange (now public on EDGAR at least 20 business days after effectiveness) and underweight/short after lock-up expiry; the paper reports lower post-IPO returns but exact magnitudes were not retrievable.
- The Wen-Zhu de-SPAC result implies a cross-sectional short on de-SPAC listings whose proxy text is unusually definitive/optimistic relative to IPO peers; sample is small (circa 115 de-SPACs) and the paper is unpublished.
- Recent ML evidence (2025 JBF, 2019–2024 measurement study) suggests prospectus-text effects on first-day returns are modest relative to deal terms and may not survive controls in recent samples; treat text as a complement to price-revision/underwriter variables.

### Gaps
- Exact coefficients, t-stats and sample sizes for Hanley-Hoberg 2012, Arnold-Fishe-North 2010, Ferris-Hao-Liao 2013 and the Lowry-Michaely-Volkova post-IPO return effect could not be verified (full texts blocked).
- No peer-reviewed study found that uses S-1 text to predict long-run (1–3 year) post-IPO returns with portfolio alphas; the FinNLP 2022 paper may do so but its text was inaccessible.
- No evidence found on follow-on offering prospectus (424B5) text and SEO returns.
- No published 2023–2026 LLM (GPT/Claude) study on S-1 text and returns was located; only ML/XGBoost on structured variables.

## Key Question 2: Proxy statements (DEF 14A): CD&A, say-on-pay, related-party transactions, board descriptions, shareholder proposals

### Takeaway
Proxy text research is mostly about governance outcomes rather than returns: harder-to-read CD&As accompany excess CEO pay and reduce say-on-pay dissent (obfuscation works), related-party-transaction disclosure is associated with lower valuations and marginally lower subsequent returns, and golden-parachute adoption is followed by negative abnormal returns. Direct evidence that CD&A tone/boilerplate predicts stock returns was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Laksmana, Tietz & Yang, "Compensation Discussion and Analysis (CD&A): Readability and Management Obfuscation," Journal of Accounting and Public Policy 2012: firms whose CEO pay exceeds benchmark pay have harder-to-read CD&As; these firms improved CD&A readability in the 2008 proxy season under regulatory/public pressure — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228237776_Compensation_Discussion_and_Analysis_CDA_Readability_and_Management_Obfuscation)
- Hooghiemstra, Kuang & Qin, "Does Obfuscating Excessive CEO Pay Work? The Influence of Remuneration Report Readability on Say-on-Pay Votes," Accounting and Business Research 47(6), 2017, pp. 695–729 (UK data): when CEO pay is mildly excessive, a less readable compensation report is associated with lower say-on-pay dissent; institutional investors show higher dissent when reports are opaque — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2900877)
- "It's OK to pay well, if you write well: The effects of remuneration disclosure readability," Journal of Business Finance & Accounting (2020): examines whether say-on-pay votes respond to CD&A readability; notes CD&A is required by SEC rules to be in plain English yet remains long and complex — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jbfa.12431)
- Kohlbeck & Mayhew, "Valuation of Firms That Disclose Related Party Transactions," Journal of Accounting and Public Policy 29(2), 2010, pp. 115–137: using 1,261 S&P 1500 firms, firms disclosing RPTs have significantly lower valuations and marginally lower subsequent returns than non-disclosers; most common RPTs were loans (now largely barred by SOX §402); disclosure location (footnote vs proxy) is driven by dollar materiality — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jappol/v29yi2p115-137.html); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/46497799_Valuation_of_Firms_That_Disclose_Related_Party_Transactions)
- Kohlbeck & Mayhew, "Do related party transactions affect family firm valuations?" JBFA 50(9-10), 2023, pp. 1984–2020: the founder-led family-firm valuation premium is reduced when such firms disclose RPTs, especially opportunistic RPTs — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jbfnac/v50y2023i9-10p1984-2020.html)
- Bebchuk, Cohen & Wang, "Golden Parachutes and the Wealth of Shareholders," Journal of Corporate Finance 2014: golden parachutes are associated with higher expected acquisition premia, but adopting firms experience a reduction in industry-adjusted Tobin's Q and negative abnormal stock returns both during the adoption inter-volume period and subsequently — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0929119913001156); [HBS draft](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/GP%20Draft%20Nov%201%202013_a771cc7c-b1bf-4f9a-ac0d-b420ef635f3c.pdf)
- Gillan, Hartzell & Parrino, "Explicit versus Implicit Contracts: Evidence from CEO Employment Agreements," Journal of Finance 2009: fewer than half of S&P 500 CEOs have comprehensive explicit employment agreements; explicit agreements are more likely and longer where relationship sustainability is less certain and the CEO's expected loss from non-performance is greater — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2009.01475.x)

### Inferences
- A testable governance-event thesis: flag DEF 14A filings whose CD&A readability drops year-over-year while reported CEO pay rises above a peer benchmark; expect lower say-on-pay dissent (vote outcome, from 8-K Item 5.07) and, per Laksmana et al., a higher probability of future disclosure remediation; no return alpha is documented.
- RPT text (Item 404 section of DEF 14A) is a candidate value screen: Kohlbeck-Mayhew's "marginally lower subsequent returns" is the only direct return evidence, and it is weak; an LLM classifier for "opportunistic" vs "ordinary-course" RPTs would mirror their 2023 taxonomy.
- Golden parachute / change-of-control provisions are extracted from DEF 14A ("Potential Payments upon Termination or Change in Control") and EX-10 employment agreements; Bebchuk et al. imply a negative drift post-adoption, but the sample is based on IRRC/ISS data rather than text extraction.

### Gaps
- No study found linking CD&A tone, pay-for-performance language or proxy boilerplate to future stock returns or earnings; the literature stops at votes, pay levels and valuation.
- No shareholder-proposal text study (e.g., proposal wording predicting vote outcomes or returns) surfaced in this search.
- No 2022–2026 LLM paper on DEF 14A text was found; a 2025 arXiv paper evaluates LLM stance detection on SEC filings and earnings calls but does not report return tests — [arXiv 2510.23464](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.23464).
- Effect sizes for Hooghiemstra et al. and the JBFA 2020 paper could not be verified (paywalled).

## Key Question 3: SEC comment letters (UPLOAD / CORRESP)

### Takeaway
Comment letters are the strongest "event + text" signal outside 10-K/10-Q: public release is followed by a small negative return and a 1–5% negative drift over the next 50 trading days (TAR 2016), with revenue-recognition letters and letter counts predicting restatements and write-downs (RAST 2021); insiders sell abnormally before release and retail investors reduce holdings in firms receiving longer, less readable letters.

### Cited Findings
- Dechow, Lawrence & Ryans, "SEC Comment Letters and Insider Sales," The Accounting Review 91(2), 2016, pp. 401–439: insider selling is significantly above normal before public disclosure of comment letters relating to revenue recognition, and triple its normal level for firms with high short interest; a small negative return at release and a negative drift of 1–5% over the following 50 days; greater pre-disclosure insider sales → stronger negative drift; comment letters are downloaded infrequently from EDGAR after release (investor inattention) — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2336812); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/272236423_SEC_Comment_Letters_and_Insider_Sales); [CFO.com coverage](https://www.cfo.com/news/sec-comment-letters-lead-insiders-to-dump-shares/665054/)
- Ryans, "Textual classification of SEC comment letters," Review of Accounting Studies 26(1), March 2021, pp. 37–80: naive Bayesian classification of letter text identifies letters associated with future restatements and write-downs; an attention-based importance measure using EDGAR downloads also predicts these outcomes; disclosure-event abnormal returns, revenue-recognition comments and the number of letters in a conversation are useful importance metrics; innocuous letters are associated with improved earnings credibility after review — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-020-09565-6); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v26y2021i1d10.1007_s11142-020-09565-6.html); [LBS working paper](https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/1268/1/comment_letter_text_20191102.pdf)
- Cassell, Dreher & Myers, "Reviewing the SEC's Review Process: 10-K Comment Letters and the Cost of Remediation," The Accounting Review 2013: larger, older, less profitable firms and firms with weak internal controls, complex operations, smaller auditors and weak governance have longer response times, more rounds to resolution and a higher probability that the letter results in a restatement — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228273971_Reviewing_the_SEC's_Review_Process_10-K_Comment_Letters_and_the_Cost_of_Remediation)
- Johnston & Petacchi, "Regulatory Oversight of Financial Reporting: Securities and Exchange Commission Comment Letters," Contemporary Accounting Research 34, 2017, pp. 1128–1155: after comment-letter resolution the adverse-selection component of bid-ask spreads declines and earnings response coefficients increase; earlier version documents reduced return volatility and volume around earnings announcements post-resolution — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Regulatory-Oversight-of-Financial-Reporting:-and-Johnston-Petacchi/684371cd9ff1acdc29f6e7c2ca13d49ab7c9a9ea)
- Bozanic, Dietrich & Johnson, "SEC Comment Letters and Firm Disclosure," Journal of Accounting and Public Policy 36(5), 2017, pp. 337–357: after receiving letters, firms disclose more and improve readability — [FSU CV listing](https://wertheim.fsu.edu/sites/default/files/2025-05/Bozanic_CV_web.pdf)
- Cunningham, Johnson, Johnson & Lisic, "The Switch-Up: An Examination of Changes in Earnings Management after Receiving SEC Comment Letters," Contemporary Accounting Research 37(2), 2020, pp. 917–944: the comment-letter process constrains accruals-based earnings management but firms on average switch to real earnings management — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/coacre/v37y2020i2p917-944.html); [GMU draft](https://somacct.gmu.edu/CJJL_9.25.15.pdf)
- "Do SEC Comment Letters Matter for Retail Investors? Evidence from Robinhood investors" (conference paper, c. 2023, authors not shown in extracted text): abnormal Google search on the dissemination date and day after; Robinhood holders reduce positions in firms receiving letters with more issues, longer letters, and more (non-core) accounting issues; number of rounds and duration do not affect retail trade; the paper notes <10% of comment letters lead to an amended filing and <3% to a restatement (citing Cassell et al. 2013, Dechow et al. 2016, Johnston & Petacchi 2017) — [conference PDF (read locally)](https://s3.amazonaws.com/amz.xcdsystem.com/DE52A724-B7F9-5299-52604A3263D5412C_abstract_File17073/851_ViewPaper_1201105708.pdf?nocache=230511130407)
- Brown, Tian & Tucker, "The Spillover Effect of SEC Comment Letters on Qualitative Corporate Disclosure: Evidence from the Risk Factor Disclosure," Contemporary Accounting Research 2018: comment letters on risk factors change peers' disclosures — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1911-3846.12414)
- "SEC comment letters on Form S-4 and M&A accounting quality," RAST 2022 — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-021-09659-9); "The role of external regulators in mergers and acquisitions: evidence from SEC comment letters," RAST 2022 — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-022-09723-y); "The spillover effect of SEC comment letters through audit firms," RAST 30(1), 2025 — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v30y2025i1d10.1007_s11142-023-09819-z.html)
- Li, "Determinants and Consequences of SEC Comment Letters: A Review," Accounting Perspectives 2025 (literature review) — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1911-3838.12405)
- Ege, Glenn & Robinson, "Unexpected SEC Resource Constraints and Comment Letter Quality," Contemporary Accounting Research 2020 — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1911-3846.12505)

### Inferences
- Construction of the core thesis: (1) ingest UPLOAD (SEC) and CORRESP (company) filings daily; (2) classify topics with an LLM or Ryans-style Bayesian classifier, flagging revenue recognition, impairment/fair value and segment issues; (3) weight by number of letters in the conversation and by EDGAR download counts (attention); (4) short/underweight flagged firms for ~50 trading days after public release. Expected drift is 1–5% per Dechow et al.; signal is strongest where pre-release insider selling is observed in Form 4 data.
- A "resolution" long thesis: Johnston-Petacchi show lower adverse selection and higher ERCs after resolution; a long position around the first earnings announcement after the "no further comments" letter is a testable variant.
- Letter length and low readability are what retail investors react to; core accounting severity is what predicts restatements. An LLM-based severity score should therefore outperform naive length measures and could exploit retail overreaction to long-but-benign letters (mean reversion long) as well as under-reaction to short-but-severe letters.
- SEC releases letters no earlier than 20 business days after review completion, so the public release date — not the letter date — is the tradable event.

### Gaps
- t-statistics, exact sample sizes and sub-period robustness for the Dechow et al. drift were not verifiable (SSRN blocked).
- Ryans 2021 does not appear to report a tradable return-based portfolio test; whether the Bayesian classifier predicts returns (as opposed to restatements) is unknown.
- No post-2016 replication showing whether the 50-day drift has decayed since comment letters became more widely scraped (e.g., Audit Analytics and news aggregators now cover them).
- No 2022–2026 LLM-based comment-letter classification paper located.

## Key Question 4: Exhibits — credit agreements and covenants (EX-10), indentures, employment agreements, change-of-control, EX-21 subsidiaries

### Takeaway
EX-10 credit agreements are the richest exhibit for a language model: Nini, Smith & Sufi show 32% of private credit agreements contain explicit capex restrictions and that firms receiving a new restriction subsequently see higher market value and operating performance; LLM extraction of covenant terms is feasible (practitioner benchmarks on 2024 SOFR deals) but no peer-reviewed alpha from covenant text was found. EX-21 has been used mainly for tax-haven and tax-rate research, with disclosure aggregation (hiding haven subsidiaries) as a governance red flag.

### Cited Findings
- Nini, Smith & Sufi, "Creditor Control Rights and Firm Investment Policy," Journal of Financial Economics 2009: in a large sample of private credit agreements between banks and public firms, 32% contain an explicit capital-expenditure restriction; restrictions are imposed as credit quality deteriorates and are at least as sensitive to borrower quality as rates, collateral or financial covenants; restrictions cause lower investment, and firms obtaining a contract with a new restriction experience subsequent increases in market value and operating performance; the restriction is usually in the negative-covenant section near the end of the agreement as a nominal dollar cap per fiscal year — [SSRN](https://www.ssrn.com/abstract=928688); [ECGI copy](https://www.ecgi.global/sites/default/files/Creditor%20Control%20Rights%20and%20Firm%20Investment%20Policy.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/314896242_Creditor_Control_Rights_and_Firm_Investment_Policy)
- Nini, Smith & Sufi, "Creditor Control Rights, Corporate Governance, and Firm Value," Review of Financial Studies 2012 (covenant violations hand-collected from 10-Q/10-K text) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228172062_Creditor_Control_Rights_Corporate_Governance_and_Firm_Value)
- Roberts & Sufi, "Control Rights and Capital Structure: An Empirical Investigation," Journal of Finance 2009 — [Wharton PDF](https://finance.wharton.upenn.edu/~mrrobert/resources/Publications/ControlRightsCapitalStructureJF2009.pdf)
- Nini (2018), "Evidence from Creditor Monitoring of Corporate Acquisitions" — [Lehigh PDF](https://www.lehigh.edu/~jms408/Nini_2018.pdf); "Debt Covenant Design and Creditor Control Rights: Evidence from Covenant Restrictiveness and Loan Outcomes" (Ohio State working paper) — [OSU PDF](https://files.fisher.osu.edu/department-finance/public/debt_covenant_design_and_creditor_control_rights_evidence_from_covenant_restrictiveness_and_loan_outcomes.pdf)
- Practitioner LLM covenant extraction: an open evaluation extracts financial covenants and key terms from 15 SEC-filed syndicated credit agreements ($150M–$5B, 2024 Term-SOFR deals) with per-field accuracy against hand labels; notes there is no public benchmark for credit-agreement financial terms and that definitions come first while covenants sit mid-document, where LLM accuracy degrades ("lost in the middle") — [GitHub covenant-extraction-eval](https://github.com/stendeze/covenant-extraction-eval); a deterministic EDGAR parser recovers Article/Section/clause structure of EX-10 credit agreements — [GitHub edgar-itemize](https://github.com/MalcolmWardlaw/edgar-itemize)
- "Capital Markets LLM Reliability Score (CM-LRS)" (arXiv 2026) benchmarks LLM extraction of issuer, principal, coupon, call schedule and key covenants from 15 public bond prospectuses filed 2024–2025 on EDGAR — [arXiv 2607.21340](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.21340); "Resource-Efficient LLM Application for Structured Transformation of Unstructured Financial Contracts" (arXiv 2025) — [arXiv 2510.23990](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.23990)
- Dyreng & Lindsey, "Using Financial Accounting Data to Examine the Effect of Foreign Operations Located in Tax Havens and Other Countries on US Multinational Firms' Tax Rates," Journal of Accounting Research 2009 (EX-21 based): firms with tax-haven operations avoid more tax — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229057148_Using_Financial_Accounting_Data_to_Examine_the_Effect_of_Foreign_Operations_Located_in_Tax_Havens_and_Other_Countries_on_US_Multinational_Firms'_Tax_Rates)
- Dyreng, Hoopes, Langetieg & Wilde, "Strategic Subsidiary Disclosure," Journal of Accounting Research 58(3), 2020, pp. 643–692: EX-21 is the most granular required disclosure of geographic footprint; firms strategically omit subsidiaries — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/joares/v58y2020i3p643-692.html); [Bauer PDF](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/departments/accy/research/documents/Dyreng-Strategic-Subsidiary-Disclosure.pdf)
- "Tax Havens and Disclosure Aggregation" (Journal of International Business Studies): firms with haven subsidiaries in EX-21 are more likely to omit corresponding country-level geographic segment disclosure; mean firm had significant subsidiaries in 1.27 haven countries in 1996 vs 2.52 in 2018 — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/272306666_Tax_Havens_and_Disclosure_Aggregation)
- Dyreng & Hanlon, "Tax Avoidance and Multinational Firm Behavior" (Brookings 2019) summarises EX-21 haven usage trends — [Brookings PDF](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/Dyreng-Hanlon-12.14.19.pdf)

### Inferences
- Testable covenant thesis (from Nini-Smith-Sufi): detect, via LLM over newly filed EX-10 credit agreements/amendments, the first appearance of a capex restriction or tightening of financial covenants; the paper's finding of subsequent increases in market value and operating performance implies a long signal for firms whose creditors newly impose investment discipline, particularly low-credit-quality borrowers. Horizons and alphas are not reported in a tradable-portfolio form.
- Covenant headroom thesis: extract covenant thresholds (leverage, interest coverage) from EX-10 and compare with XBRL financials to estimate distance-to-violation; Nini-Smith-Sufi 2012 show violations trigger creditor control; a text-derived "near-violation" screen is a natural extension but no study was found that trades it.
- EX-21 geographic exposure: subsidiary-country lists can proxy for country/tariff/sanction exposure and tax-haven intensity; the documented link is to cash ETRs and disclosure opacity, not to returns.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed paper found that uses LLM-extracted covenant text to predict stock or bond returns (2022–2026); evidence is practitioner benchmarks only.
- No return-based study found on indenture text, employment-agreement text or change-of-control provisions extracted from EX-10 exhibits (Gillan et al. 2009 hand-code agreements but study contract design, not returns).
- No evidence found linking EX-21 subsidiary changes (e.g., new China/haven subsidiaries) to subsequent returns.

## Key Question 5: Earnings call transcripts (not SEC filings) — what a filings-only approach misses

### Takeaway
Call transcripts carry tone, deception and vocal-affect signals that predict post-announcement drift, fraud and volatility; GPT-generated risk summaries from transcripts predict volatility and returns. A filings-only pipeline misses Q&A spontaneity and vocal cues that have no analogue in SEC text.

### Cited Findings
- Mayew & Venkatachalam, "The Power of Voice: Managerial Affective States and Future Firm Performance," Journal of Finance 2012: vocal-emotion cues from call audio are incremental to earnings and linguistic content; positive (negative) affect is positively (negatively) associated with contemporaneous returns and future profitability — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2011.01705.x)
- Larcker & Zakolyukina, "Detecting Deceptive Discussions in Conference Calls," Journal of Accounting Research 50, 2012, pp. 495–540: CEO/CFO-narrative models beat random out-of-sample by 6–16% and match or dominate accounting-variable models; deceptive CEOs use more extreme positive emotion and fewer anxiety words — [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/detecting-deceptive-discussions-conference-calls); [JAR PDF](http://www.globalhha.com/doclib/data/upload/doc_con/62130899e7769.pdf)
- Price, Doran, Peterson & Bliss, "Earnings Conference Calls and Stock Returns: The Incremental Informativeness of Textual Tone," Journal of Banking & Finance 36(4), 2012, pp. 992–1011: call tone dominates earnings surprise over the 60 trading days after the call; Q&A tone has incremental power for PEAD; a context-specific dictionary beats Harvard IV-4 — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbfina/v36y2012i4p992-1011.html)
- Kim, Muhn & Nikolaev, "From Transcripts to Insights: Uncovering Corporate Risks Using Generative AI," Chicago Booth Paper 23-19 (2023): GPT-generated political, climate and AI risk exposures from transcripts — [Tilburg PDF (blocked)](https://www.tilburguniversity.edu/sites/default/files/download/Kim,%20Muhn,%20Nikolaev%20-%20From%20Transcripts%20to%20Insights.pdf)
- Kim, Muhn & Nikolaev, "Bloated Disclosures: Can ChatGPT Help Investors Process Information?" (2023): GPT summaries of MD&A and call transcripts are >70% shorter yet explain market reactions better; "bloat" (original length minus optimal summary length) is associated with lower price efficiency and higher information asymmetry and is higher for loss/negative-sentiment firms — [BFI summary](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/insight/research-summary/bloated-disclosures-can-chatgpt-help-investors-process-information/); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/equation-how-chatgpt-revealed-bloat-corporate-disclosures); [FMA PDF](https://www.fmaconferences.org/Texas/Papers/1201.pdf)
- Druz, Petzev, Wagner & Zeckhauser, "When Managers Change Their Tone, Analysts and Investors Change Their Tune" (HKS 2020) — [HKS PDF](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/HKSEE/HKSEE%20PDFs/DruzPetzevWagnerZeckhauser_Tone_2020.pdf)

### Inferences
- The "bloat" measure is directly portable to S-1 risk factors, CD&A and CORRESP responses: ask an LLM for the optimal-length summary and treat excess length as an obfuscation signal, consistent with the CD&A-readability and comment-letter-length findings above.

### Gaps
- Portfolio alphas and t-stats for the GPT risk measures in "From Transcripts to Insights" were not retrievable (PDF host blocked).

## Key Question 6: Foreign private issuers (20-F, 6-K) and small-company forms (SRC 10-K, Form D, Reg A 1-A, Form C)

### Takeaway
Only one 20-F text study was found (tone and earnings management in US-listed Chinese firms, 2002–2014); no return-prediction evidence exists for 6-K, Form D, Form 1-A or Form C text. SEC DERA publishes structured Form C and Reg A data but no textual return studies.

### Cited Findings
- Li, Wang & Luo, "Tone of language, financial disclosure, and earnings management: a textual analysis of form 20-F," Financial Innovation 8, 2022: 153 US-listed Chinese firms, 2002–2014; the proportion of positive, uncertain or modal words is positively related to earnings management, consistent with tone being used to conceal manipulation — [SpringerOpen](https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40854-022-00346-5)
- SEC DERA, "Trends in the Foreign Private Issuer Population 2003–2023" (descriptive, no text analysis) — [SEC PDF](https://www.sec.gov/files/dera_wp_fpi-trends-2412.pdf)
- SEC DERA, "Analysis of Crowdfunding" (Reg CF offerings): as of Dec 31, 2024, 3,869 offerings reported proceeds on Form C-U, aggregate about $1.3 billion, average successful offering about $346,000 — [SEC PDF](https://www.sec.gov/files/dera-reg-cf-2505.pdf); SEC Crowdfunding Offerings Data Sets (XML portion of Form C) — [SEC data page](https://www.sec.gov/data-research/sec-markets-data/crowdfunding-offerings-data-sets)
- SEC 2019 Report to the Commission on Regulation Crowdfunding (descriptive) — [SEC PDF](https://www.sec.gov/files/regulation-crowdfunding-2019_0.pdf); Reg A statistics: as of Dec 31, 2018, 91% of qualified Reg A offerings sought up to $1 million and 73% up to $5 million (per search summary of SEC material)

### Inferences
- 20-F tone-to-earnings-management mapping suggests an LLM "tone vs. fundamentals divergence" screen for FPIs (especially China-based ADRs) as a fraud/restatement risk flag, but no return test exists.
- Form D filings by public micro-caps (PIPEs) are a dilution event; combining Form D with the text of the related 8-K/EX-10 purchase agreements (warrant coverage, reset provisions) is a plausible short screen, but no academic evidence was found.

### Gaps
- No studies located on 6-K text, Form 1-A offering-circular text, Form C narrative text, or SRC-specific 10-K text predicting returns or outcomes.
- No evidence found on Form D private-placement text and public-company returns.

## Key Question 7: Investment company and fund filings (N-CSR shareholder letters, N-1A prospectuses, N-PORT)

### Takeaway
Fund text predicts flows robustly and performance weakly: negative tone in N-CSR shareholder letters reduces next-month flows (about $3.08M per one-SD for an average fund) and predicts lower idiosyncratic risk-taking; a personal writing style predicts better performance; prospectus (N-1A) risk-disclosure updates by outperforming funds predict alpha, and "informative" (non-boilerplate) prospectus content is associated with higher risk-adjusted performance. Funds that deviate from their stated strategy text lose flows.

### Cited Findings
- Hillert, Niessen-Ruenzi & Ruenzi, "Mutual Fund Shareholder Letters: Flows, Performance, and Managerial Behavior," Management Science (2024, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2021.03417; earlier SAFE working paper "Mutual Fund Shareholder Letter Tone — Do Investors Listen?"): less negative tone and a personal writing style raise net flows; a one-SD increase in the fraction of negative words lowers next-month flows by $3.08 million for an average-size fund; no stable relation between tone and future performance, but negative tone strongly predicts less idiosyncratic risk-taking; personal style predicts better performance; dishonest communication inconsistent with actual performance reduces flows — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2021.03417); [SAFE summary](https://safe-frankfurt.de/news-latest/all-news/news-view/tone-of-shareholder-letters-influences-capital-flows-into-mutual-funds.html); [UZH PDF](https://phd-finance.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:7b4c6dc7-ee25-4f96-a028-65ff793fe8c7/FS_spring18_paper_Niessen-Ruenzi.pdf); [CFR Cologne PDF](https://www.cfr-cologne.de/download/kolloquium/2015/HillertNiessen-RuenziRuenzi.pdf)
- Abis & Lines, "Do Mutual Funds Keep Their Promises?" (Columbia working paper, 2021) and "Broken promises, competition, and capital allocation in the mutual fund industry," Journal of Financial Economics 2024: machine-learning clustering of prospectus strategy descriptions; funds largely keep their promises; when a fund diverges from its text-defined group's core strategy, investors withdraw capital — [Columbia](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/do-mutual-funds-keep-their-promises); [ScienceDirect JFE](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X24001715)
- "Mutual Funds and Risk Disclosure: Information Content of Fund Prospectuses" (EFMA 2020/2021 conference paper; likely the Krakow & Schäfer "Mutual Funds and Qualitative Disclosure" SSRN paper — authorship not verified): decomposes prospectus text into informative vs standard content; fund risk-taking and risk-adjusted performance increase with informativeness; regular updates of disclosed risks have predictive power for alpha — updates by outperforming funds carry positive information about future performance, while underperformers show a weaker negative effect — [EFMA PDF](https://www.efmaefm.org/0EFMAMEETINGS/EFMA%20ANNUAL%20MEETINGS/2021-Leeds/papers/EFMA%202020_stage-1301_question-Full%20Paper_id-351.pdf); [SSRN Krakow & Schäfer](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3648089)
- Mitali, "How Do Mutual Fund Investors React to Text-Based Uncertainty?" (JMP, Nov 2022): fund flows respond strongly to text-based measures in prospectuses; a more readable, less ambiguous style strengthens the flow effect — [author PDF](https://sfmitali.gitlab.io/202211_jmp_paper.pdf)
- "Empty ESG Promises: A Text Analysis of Mutual Fund Prospectuses" (Papakonstantinou et al., working paper) — [author PDF](https://www.filippospapakonstantinou.com/ESG_Prospectus.pdf); "Spotting Portfolio Greenwashing in Environmental Funds," Journal of Business Ethics 2024 — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10551-024-05783-z)
- MIT Sloan study: fees are positively associated with narrative and structural complexity of fund disclosures, consistent with complexity obfuscating high fees — [MIT Sloan](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/study-finds-mutual-fund-disclosures-are-unnecessarily-complex)
- "Practice What You Preach: Strategy Consistency and Mutual Fund Performance" (Athena Invest, 2022, practitioner) — [PDF](https://athenainvest.com/wp-content/uploads/Strategy_Consistency_Mutual_Fund_Performance_2022_08.pdf)

### Inferences
- Fund-selection thesis: score N-1A (485BPOS) prospectus risk sections for year-over-year updates and informativeness relative to peers; favour outperforming funds that materially update risk text (positive alpha signal per the EFMA paper) and avoid funds whose holdings (N-PORT) drift from their strategy text (flow outflows per Abis-Lines).
- Flow-prediction thesis: N-CSR letter negative tone → lower next-month flows; useful for predicting forced selling/liquidity in concentrated holdings, but tone does not predict fund alpha.

### Gaps
- Exact alpha magnitudes, t-stats and sample periods for the prospectus risk-update result were not verifiable (EFMA/SSRN blocked) and authorship of the EFMA paper is unconfirmed.
- No study found that uses N-PORT narrative fields or N-CSR text to predict returns of the underlying stocks.

## Key Question 8: Registration, going-concern, deregistration and amendment forms (Form 15, Form 25, S-3 shelf/ATM, S-8, 10-K/A, 8-K/A)

### Takeaway
Form 15 "going dark" is followed by roughly a 10% negative abnormal return (JAE 2008); S-3 shelf filings show a mean one-day reaction around -1.5% in a 268-filing biotech sample (practitioner data, 2008–2012); no peer-reviewed text-based evidence was found for S-8, 10-K/A or 8-K/A, although late 10-K filers show continued negative post-filing drift.

### Cited Findings
- Leuz, Triantis & Wang, "Why do firms go dark? Causes and economic consequences of voluntary SEC deregistrations," Journal of Accounting and Economics 45(2-3), 2008, pp. 181–208: firms deregistering (Form 15) while continuing to trade experience large negative abnormal returns, about a 10% drop after the initial announcement and filing; going dark spiked after SOX and is driven by poor prospects, distress and compliance costs; minority shareholders view going dark as serving insiders — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jaecon/v45y2008i2-3p181-208.html); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227406414_Why_do_firms_go_dark)
- Doidge, Karolyi & Stulz, "Why Do Foreign Firms Leave U.S. Equity Markets?" Journal of Finance 65(4), 2010 (Form 15F / Form 25 deregistrations by foreign firms) — [OSU PDF](https://bpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/u.osu.edu/dist/0/30211/files/2017/01/why-do-foreign-firms-leave-the-US-Equity-Markets-sh2s93.pdf)
- Practitioner biotech data (Nature "Trade Secrets" blog, Bruce Booth 2012–2013): for 268 new S-3 filings Q2 2008–Q2 2012, mean one-day share-price change after filing was -1.5% (range -24.3% to +34.3%); 215 of 268 subsequently raised money, 44% at prices above the S-3-date price — [S-3 Barometer](https://blogs.nature.com/tradesecrets/2012/11/16/the-s-3-barometer); [More on the Shelf](https://blogs.nature.com/tradesecrets/2013/02/26/more-on-the-shelf)
- Bartov, DeFond & Konchitchki, "Capital Market Consequences of Filing Late 10-Qs and 10-Ks": late 10-K filers' abnormal returns continue to drift downward in post-filing months, less so when accounting reasons explain the delay — [Bocconi presentation PDF](https://accounting.unibocconi.eu/sites/default/files/files/media/attachments/BDK_BocconiPresentation_June201220120605171817.pdf)
- You & Zhang, "Financial Reporting Complexity and Investor Underreaction to 10-K Information," Review of Accounting Studies 2009: 12-month post-10-K drift stronger for complex reports (context for amendments) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-008-9083-2)
- Form S-8 is effective on filing and cannot be used for capital raising; no return studies surfaced — [SEC Lawyer 101 guide](https://www.securitieslawyer101.com/2025/07/07/what-is-form-s-8/); [PwC Viewpoint](https://viewpoint.pwc.com/dt/us/en/sec/cdis/cdis_US/securities_act_forms__1_US/questions_and_answer__15_US/section_126_form_s8_US.html)

### Inferences
- Form 15 is an event signal, not a text signal: the ~10% drop occurs at announcement, so a text model could only add value by predicting which firms will go dark (small, distressed, high compliance cost per Leuz et al.).
- S-3/ATM: the -1.5% mean day-one reaction with wide dispersion suggests text of the prospectus supplement (424B5, ATM "sales agreement" EX-1/EX-10) could sort likely dilution size, but no study tests this.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found on 10-K/A or 8-K/A as a text or event signal for returns (search returned only general 10-K filing-response literature).
- No academic study found on S-8 filings and returns, nor on ATM program announcements in a broad sample.
- No text-based study of Form 25 (exchange delisting) was located.

## Key Question 9: Litigation and regulatory text (SEC litigation releases, AAERs, 10-K Item 3 legal proceedings)

### Takeaway
Legal-proceedings text modifications in 10-Ks predict future litigation, and AAER-labelled fraud firms exhibit distinctive MD&A language that SVM/BERT/LLM classifiers can detect out-of-sample; direct evidence that litigation-release or AAER text predicts returns was not found (AAERs are mostly used as labels, not as predictive text).

### Cited Findings
- Li, "Litigation-Disclosure Modifications in the 10-K: Large-Sample Evidence," Journal of Accounting, Auditing & Finance 2023: modifications to Item 103 legal proceedings and contingency-note disclosures predict future occurrence of litigation cases — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0148558X211017838)
- Ganguly, "Textual Disclosure in SEC Filings and Litigation Risk" (Univ. of Pittsburgh dissertation): positive association between the extent of textual disclosure in 10-Ks and 10-Qs and securities class-action risk — [Pitt PDF](https://d-scholarship.pitt.edu/35206/1/Dissertation_Arup_Final.pdf)
- Purda & Skillicorn, "Accounting Variables, Deception, and a Bag of Words: Assessing the Tools of Fraud Detection," Contemporary Accounting Research 2015: SVM on the top-200 fraud-linked MD&A words identifies AAER fraud; fraudulent firms use more positive and fewer negative words — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228240037_Accounting_Variables_Deception_and_a_Bag_of_Words_Assessing_the_Tools_of_Fraud_Detection)
- Brown, Crowley & Elliott, "What are you saying? Using topic to detect financial misreporting," Journal of Accounting Research 2020 (topic models on 10-K text vs AAER/restatement labels) — [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/3526/edfec08aa0c44808541d6a8a1b3ee55c81b6.pdf)
- "Accounting fraud detection using contextual language learning," International Journal of Accounting Information Systems 2024 (BERT on 10-K text, AAER labels) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1467089524000150); "Improving Financial Statement Fraud Detection: A Large Language Model Processing Approach" (ACM 2025/2026) — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3796514); "Artificial Intelligence in fraud detection: textual analysis of 10-K filings" (2025) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/392594631_Artificial_Intelligence_in_fraud_detection_textual_analysis_of_10-K_filings)
- The SEC itself maps topic and tonality signals from filings into risk factors using ML/NLP (per the fraud-detection literature review cited above) — [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/3526/edfec08aa0c44808541d6a8a1b3ee55c81b6.pdf)

### Inferences
- A fraud-short thesis would train an LLM classifier on MD&A/Item 3/CORRESP text with AAER and restatement labels; Larcker-Zakolyukina-style out-of-sample lift (6–16% over random on transcripts) and Purda-Skillicorn's SVM results suggest the signal is real but AAER base rates are very low, so expected hit rates and returns are untested.

### Gaps
- No study found that treats the text of SEC litigation releases or AAERs themselves (as opposed to using them as labels) as a return predictor.
- No portfolio-return evidence for Item 3 text modifications.

## Key Question 10: Ranking of forms by tradable evidence and cross-cutting 2022–2026 LLM work

### Takeaway
Ordered by tradable, return-based evidence: (1) S-1/424B4, (2) comment letters, (3) EX-10 credit agreements, (4) DEF 14A, (5) fund N-CSR/N-1A, (6) de-SPAC proxies, (7) Form 15 (event only), (8) everything else (S-3/ATM, 10-K/A, S-8, Form D, 1-A, Form C, 20-F/6-K, AAER text) where evidence is thin or absent. Published 2022–2026 LLM work concentrates on 10-K MD&A and transcripts (Kim-Muhn-Nikolaev), SPAC vs IPO text (Wen-Zhu, dictionary-based), fraud detection with BERT/LLMs, and practitioner covenant-extraction benchmarks; no peer-reviewed LLM study on S-1, DEF 14A or comment-letter text with return tests was found.

### Cited Findings
- Ranking inputs: S-1 text effects of 3–4% (one-SD uncertain/negative) and 4%/8% (standard/informative content) on first-day returns — [L&M 2013](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256030728_IPO_First-Day_Returns_Offer_Price_Revisions_Volatility_and_Form_S-1_Language); [Hanley-Hoberg 2010](https://www.jstor.org/stable/40782968); comment-letter 1–5% 50-day drift — [Dechow et al. 2016](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2336812); capex-covenant imposition followed by higher market value — [Nini-Smith-Sufi](https://www.ssrn.com/abstract=928688); RPT disclosure → lower valuation and marginally lower returns — [Kohlbeck-Mayhew 2010](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jappol/v29yi2p115-137.html); golden parachutes → negative abnormal returns — [Bebchuk et al. 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0929119913001156); fund letter tone → flows ($3.08M per SD) — [Hillert et al.](https://safe-frankfurt.de/news-latest/all-news/news-view/tone-of-shareholder-letters-influences-capital-flows-into-mutual-funds.html); de-SPAC text → larger price declines — [Wen-Zhu 2022](https://www.sec.gov/comments/s7-13-22/s71322-20130553-299411.pdf); Form 15 → ~10% drop — [Leuz et al. 2008](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jaecon/v45y2008i2-3p181-208.html)
- LLM-era references: Bloated Disclosures (2023) — [BFI](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/insight/research-summary/bloated-disclosures-can-chatgpt-help-investors-process-information/); From Transcripts to Insights (2023) — [Tilburg PDF](https://www.tilburguniversity.edu/sites/default/files/download/Kim,%20Muhn,%20Nikolaev%20-%20From%20Transcripts%20to%20Insights.pdf); Lopez-Lira & Tang, "Can ChatGPT Forecast Stock Price Movements?" (2023/2024, news headlines, not filings) — [UCLA PDF](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/sites/default/files/document/2024-04/4.19.24%20Alejandro%20Lopez%20Lira%20ChatGPT_V3.pdf); "A Survey of Large Language Models for Financial Applications" (2024) — [arXiv 2406.11903](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.11903); "Evaluating LLMs in Financial NLP" (2025) — [arXiv 2507.22936](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2507.22936); SECQUE benchmark for SEC-filing QA (2025) — [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2025.gem-1.16.pdf); "Beyond the Reported Cutoff: Where LLMs Fall Short on Financial Knowledge" (2025) — [arXiv 2504.00042](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.00042); LLM stance detection on SEC filings and calls (2025) — [arXiv 2510.23464](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.23464)
- Textual-tool references usable across forms: Loughran-McDonald word lists (uncertain, weak modal, negative, positive, legal, strong modal) — [L&M 2013](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256030728_IPO_First-Day_Returns_Offer_Price_Revisions_Volatility_and_Form_S-1_Language); Jegadeesh & Wu "Word Power" market-weighted lexicon — [Penn PDF](https://repository.upenn.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/caf9d0c9-0de5-475a-ac72-f6e4ad3bfce5/content); EDGAR structure parser for exhibits — [edgar-itemize](https://github.com/MalcolmWardlaw/edgar-itemize)

### Inferences
- Highest expected practical value for an LLM pipeline, given the evidence: (a) comment-letter severity scoring (CORRESP/UPLOAD) with a 50-day short horizon; (b) S-1 uncertainty/boilerplate scoring for IPO positioning; (c) EX-10 covenant extraction for distance-to-violation and new-restriction screens; (d) DEF 14A RPT/golden-parachute extraction as value/governance overlays; (e) N-1A risk-update and strategy-drift scoring for fund selection.
- Decay evidence is essentially absent for every form in this scope; all headline results come from pre-2016 samples (S-1: 1997–2010 era; comment letters: 2005–2012 era), so out-of-sample re-estimation on 2016–2025 data is a necessary first step.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed 2022–2026 LLM study with return tests on S-1, DEF 14A, CORRESP/UPLOAD, EX-10, EX-21, N-CSR or Form 15 text was located; the LLM literature in scope is either benchmark-only or focused on 10-K/transcripts.
- Decay/robustness studies for the Dechow et al. drift and the Loughran-McDonald S-1 effects were not found.
- Many primary PDFs were inaccessible through the proxy (SSRN, Wiley, Springer, INFORMS, Fed, RePEc, eScholarship, Lehigh, Tilburg, EFMA, gitlab.io), so t-statistics and holding-period details are missing for most entries above and should be pulled by the report writer or a follow-up pass with direct access.
